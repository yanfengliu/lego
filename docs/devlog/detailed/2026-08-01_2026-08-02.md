## A panel's camera is fitted from geometry already known, not guessed

**Timestamp:** 2026-08-01

**Action:** Built a camera fitter for instruction panels. Four of the six parameters are solved rather than searched: once a direction is chosen, scale and image-plane offset follow in closed form from the two silhouettes' areas and centroids, leaving a plain sweep over azimuth and elevation. Roll is not a parameter. The fitter takes a render callback rather than a renderer.

**Code reviewer comments:** None recorded.

**Result:** End to end on six catalog parts through the real rasteriser: recovered a view it was never told to within 0.22 degrees of azimuth, 0.06 of elevation, 0.09 px of centre and 0.09% of scale — IoU 0.9978, 1231 renders in 5.3 s. Seeded at the answer it scores exactly 1.0000. +23 unit tests, +1 Playwright probe; verify green at 584 unit, 21 browser.

**Reasoning:** At step N the model built so far is known exactly, so recovering a panel's view is an optimisation against known geometry rather than a guess, and because every panel re-calibrates from scratch, camera drift cannot accumulate across 359 steps. Equal-area rather than bounding-box, because a panel shows parts not yet placed and one stray part moves a box by its own width while moving the area by a percent. Printed panels are axis-aligned, so a roll term only helps a fit lie. Taking a render callback lets the search be unit-tested against an analytic projection with no graphics context while the same code drives real WebGL in the probe.

**Validation:** Analytic-projection unit tests plus a WebGL Playwright probe that paints target, fit, and their disagreement.

**Notes:** Three things the numbers only gave up under measurement. One alignment pass is exact only when the trial render is unclipped — a seed at twice the scale in the opposite corner measures a truncated area and picks the wrong direction outright: 0.807 at three passes, 0.976 at five, so the default is six with an early exit. IoU has a resolution ceiling: a correct fit on a 290-pixel target scores 0.90, so a score is only comparable against another measured at the same scale, and a test pins that ceiling. Refinement resolution is the fit's error floor — five passes take a 15-degree sweep to 0.47 degrees, and half a degree of residual costs several points of IoU on a small target. Painting the disagreement is what caught three canvases stacked at one position, which had screenshotted as three copies of the top one.

## The search is stored as a tree of deltas, not a tree of states

**Timestamp:** 2026-08-01

**Action:** A search node is content-addressed by its parent and its placement. Identical work anywhere in the tree dedupes to one node; two children of one parent are two branches, so a rejected branch survives as counterevidence; backtracking moves a head pointer and deletes nothing. No I/O — the tree is a value and `encodeNode` is a line of JSON.

**Code reviewer comments:** None recorded.

**Result:** A logged node is 418 bytes, three quarters of it the three sha256 hex digests it carries. That is four times the back-of-envelope hundred bytes and leaves the conclusion untouched. +12 unit tests; verify green at 622 unit, 22 browser.

**Reasoning:** Step N differs from step N-1 by one placement. Content addressing buys dedup, branch retention and non-destructive backtracking structurally rather than by remembering to preserve them. The same structure serves a browser search, a JSON-lines append log, and the companion's run ledger without any of them being wired in.

**Validation:** Replay verifies itself — an id is a content address, so a line whose parent and placement no longer digest to it was altered after it was written, and a test tampers with one to prove the check fires.

**Notes:** Also replaced the branching-factor test's wall-clock bound with the property it was trying to assert. **Reasoning:** The first attempt put an `accepts` list on every kind, the way the existing ports did. ## Parts that move together are one rigid body, and articulated pairs are boundaries

**Timestamp:** 2026-08-01

**Action:** `deriveAssemblies` splits a document into rigid components by union-find over its valid connections, treating articulated pairs as boundaries. Valid connections are a required argument, not read from the document; `validBrickConnections` exposes the validator's own list for callers.

**Code reviewer comments:** None recorded.

**Result:** Six tests, including the one the specification asks for by name — removing the part bridging two halves splits the assembly. Verify green at 653 unit, 24 browser.

**Reasoning:** A physics engine wants one body per group of parts that cannot move relative to each other and one constraint per place they can. A 1465-piece model held together by studs is one rigid thing; simulating it as 1465 bodies joined by thousands of constraints is slower and less stable. ## The booklet's arrows are censused, and one arrow is worth a twentieth of a stud

**Timestamp:** 2026-08-02

**Action:** Fixed three defects in the arrow reader, then measured what an arrow is worth. Added `distanceToMask` to `panel-difference`, which `boundaryOffset` already needed and the clearance measurement now shares.

**Code reviewer comments:** None recorded; two of the three were found by looking at panels.

**Result:** An empty origin mask had been treated as a failed constraint rather than an absent one — steps 1 and 38 close no highlight contour at all, because the first step of a build rings nothing, and their arrows of about 500px each were rejected as belonging to a sub-build that does not exist. The skip path reported zeros for the arrow facts, so a step that printed a perfectly good arrow and then failed on a missing camera came back as a step that printed no arrow. The third had the same shape and cost more: the on-model test and the clearance measurement were computed after the skips too, so the count of arrows drawn on the model went from 3 to 11 once they were measured before the skips. The census: 13 of the first 50 steps print an arrow that survives the shape and origin tests — 1, 2, 10, 12, 13, 16, 32, 35, 38, 40, 45, 48, 49 — and 11 of those draw it on the model rather than on a sub-build strip. Arrows on one step agree to between 0.5 and 4.0 px, median 1.0, which at 21 to 43 px per stud is a few hundredths. Each is systematically short, drawn clear of the ghost at one end and clear of the landing surface at the other: the two gaps come to 0.00, 0.05, 0.38 and 0.47 of a stud on the four steps with a fitted camera, a median of 0.22. So the arrow is worth a fifth of a stud raw and a twentieth corrected — an order of magnitude sharper than the panel difference it was brought in to check.

**Reasoning:** Separating sub-build strips matters because this booklet draws sub-builds as numbered panels inside a step's panel and rings each sub-step in yellow exactly like a main step: "starts at something this step highlighted" does not tell them apart and "the tail sits on the assembly" does. Step 48 gives itself away in its own numbers — an arrow head 253px from the nearest built model at the end of a 29px arrow is not spanning a gap in that model. The bias is a bias to subtract, not noise to live with, because both gaps are readable off the same pixels as the arrow.

**Validation:** Census over the first fifty steps of the real booklet, with per-step numbers.

**Notes:** A measurement computed after an early return reports zero for everything the early return skipped, and the census then answers a different question from the one asked ("how many steps got all the way through" rather than "how many steps print an arrow"). Recorded as a lesson. The arrow-to-world conversion carried a separate defect nobody caught here: every displacement in the repository was half the true travel, fixed on 2026-08-07.

## A step's part is named by matching its printed drawing to the inventory the book prints at the back

**Timestamp:** 2026-08-02

**Action:** Geometry proposes a shortlist, a vision call picks from it, and the printed inventory disposes. The pick must be a number on the card, must name a listed element, and must agree with the free description the same call gave — and the call never sees the published names. The choice is then made once for the whole book as a minimum-cost assignment under one-element-one-drawing.

**Code reviewer comments:** None recorded.

**Result:** Measured on `recipes/6651557.pdf`, 870 callouts in 273 distinct drawings. Nearest match, geometry only: 1256/1465 pieces, 174/276 elements exact. Global assignment, geometry: 1308/1465 pieces, 203/276 elements exact. Plus a Sonnet pick: 1300/1465 pieces, 199/276 elements exact. On the first fifty steps, judged by eye against each element's own printed drawing, per-callout accuracy is 158/186 (84.9%) with the vision pick and 140/186 (75.3%) without. Haiku is not usable here: it contradicted its own pick on 214 of 265 drawings and the check threw them out.

**Reasoning:** The grader is the book — every callout summed per element must come to the 276 elements and 1465 pieces printed at the back, which scores all 359 steps at once with nothing hand-labelled, and it is falsifiable. Global assignment beats per-item matching because taking an element costs every other drawing the chance to take it. Pairing the vision call's free answer with its closed-set answer means the model cannot certify itself.

**Validation:** Conservation against the printed inventory, plus 186 hand-judged callouts over the first fifty steps.

**Notes:** Artifacts, contact sheets and the judged pairs are under `output/` (gitignored). `@napi-rs/canvas` is loaded on demand with a named error, because it reaches this checkout only under `pdfjs-dist` and is not a declared dependency. Three lessons were recorded from this work: global assignment against per-item matching, colour as the term that separates two element ids sharing one shape, and pairing a vision call's free answer with its closed-set answer.

## An arrow hands physics a shortlist of two to four placements, and the tolerance that decides is the plate quantum

**Code reviewer comments:** None recorded.

**Result:** Inverting a pixel displacement onto the brick grid is underdetermined — two numbers, three axes — and how underdetermined turns on one tolerance. A plate of height projects to about a third of a stud on this booklet's cameras (0.322 to 0.330 across every panel that fitted one), so a tolerance at or above that admits the layer above and below by construction: at 0.35 of a stud the booklet's arrows admit 12 to 18 whole-grid displacements apiece. **Reasoning:** The default is chosen against the quantum, not the noise, and the module says so. The arrow does not place a part; it hands physics a shortlist.

**Validation:** Measured against every panel that fitted a camera in the first fifty steps.

**Notes:** A third shape test does not work and is kept with that written down.
